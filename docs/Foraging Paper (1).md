**SUMMARY (250 words)**

Human childhood is characterized by slow growth and extensive parental and alloparental care, including food provisioning (Kaplan, Kramer, Bogin, Gettler). It is hypothesized to have evolved as an extended developmental period that allows for the metabolic demands of brain growth and the acquisition of cognitive and physical skills needed to successfully exploit the high-quality difficult-to-acquire hunted and gathered resources typical of our species' subsistence niche (Kaplan, Kraft, Kuzawa). Studies in contemporary hunter-gatherer communities indeed suggest that children's foraging proficiency increases with age, with complex resources production peaking in adulthood (Kaplan, Crittenden, Pretelli, Gurven, Koster). Yet, some young foragers can exceed their daily caloric needs (Crittenden, Tucker, Kramer, Bird). Little attention has been paid to inter-individual variation in *effort* as a potential explanation for these contradictory findings. The BaYaka are foragers who rely on hunting, gathering, fishing, and small-scale horticulture for subsistence. **RESULTS**  
BaYaka are immediate return hunter-gatherers who highly value egalitarianism, generalized sharing, and individual autonomy (Lewis, Kitanishi, Boyette). This autonomy is extended to children, whose self-directed learning primarily occurs in the multi-aged mixed-gender peer group where they teach, play, and forage together (Lew-Levy a,b,c, Lew-Levy spear, Salali, Boyette, Karline). Importantly for the present paper, BaYaka children are granted the autonomy to decide whether or not they will participate in foraging on any given day (Boyette, Lew-Levy). Children may refuse their parents' requests to accompany them on a foraging trip, while parents may also feel obligated to allow children to join even when they would prefer otherwise (Boyette, Lew-Levy c). Therefore, BaYaka children's foraging effort reflects their own motivation, rather than others' expectations.

Most previous research on hunter-gatherer children's foraging has focused on production rates for specific resources (e.g., gram/hour) (Pretelli), such as tubers (Tucker), game (Bird 2005), and fish (Bliege Bird 2002a; Bock 2005). By considering children's daily foraging production (Crittenden), here we account for effort in collecting the full breadth of resources on which BaYaka rely. Further, foraging is often a cooperative activity involving participants with varying skill levels (Morin, Jangx3). Accordingly, we documented all individuals involved in the collection of each food package in order to account for relative contributions to foraging success (Koster). Since foraging is often opportunistic (e.g., collecting mushrooms discovered while searching for firewood), we considered days involving time spent outside of camp as reflecting foraging effort. Because hunter-gatherers of all ages consume a substantial portion of food during foraging trips (Berbesque, Crittenden), ignoring out-of-camp consumption risks underestimating foraging production. We therefore asked participants to recall what and how much they collected and consumed during their foraging trip. Finally, variation in energy per unit mass across diverse resource types (Schnorr); we thus standardized foraging production to kilocalories (kcals) (see Benoit for more detail). 

*Definitions and operationalizations:* We define foraging skill as an individual's underlying ability to successfully acquire food, which we model as an age-dependent latent variable. Foraging effort is operationalized as daily participation (binary: did the individual leave camp to forage on a given day). Foraging returns are measured at the group level in kilocalories.

*Statistical approach:* We used a Bayesian hierarchical model with three components: binary effort, binary success, and group-level returns (see Methods for details). The model estimates correlated random effects for effort and skill, allowing us to test whether lower-skilled individuals compensate through increased effort.

**Foraging skill increases with age**

Individual foraging skill increased with age following a learning-senescence curve that peaked at approximately 57 years (95% CI: 38-93). Foragers reached 50% of their peak skill by age 11.5 years (95% CI: 3-23), indicating that substantial skill acquisition occurs during childhood and adolescence, with continued improvement into middle adulthood.

**Foraging effort increases with age**

The probability of going foraging on any given day increased with age, with a positive linear effect (β = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.37-1.07) and a negative quadratic effect (β = -0.68, 95% CI: -0.90 to -0.45). This indicates an inverted-U shaped relationship: effort increases from childhood through adulthood, peaks in middle age, and then declines among older foragers.

**Lower skilled individuals expend more foraging effort**

We found a negative correlation between individual random effects for effort and skill (r = -0.30, 95% CI: -0.66 to 0.09), with 93% posterior probability that the correlation is negative. This suggests that individuals with lower foraging skill compensate by increasing their foraging effort—going out to forage more frequently than their higher-skilled peers.

**METHODS**

**Approvals**  
Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Cambridge Department of Psychology Research Ethics Committee (PRE.2018.023). In-country permission was received from the Institute de Recherche en Sciences Exactes et Naturelles (IRSEN). During a camp-wide meeting prior to the start of data collection, we outlined the study aims, data collection procedures, and gifts to be received at the end of the stay. We explained that any and all camp members could decline to participate, or stop their participation at any time. We answered any questions camp members had. The camp decided by consensus that we could conduct research. We then sought individual free, prior, and informed consent. Verbal assent for adults and parents/guardians, and child assent, was obtained for all participants. 

**Study population**  
BaYaka live in the dense tropical forest of the Congo Basin. BaYaka inhabit diverse socioecologies, including remote forest camps, market-integrated logging towns, and large cities (Salali; Lew-Levy, Rendell, other). In the present study, we worked with BaYaka from a remote village in the northern Republic of the Congo. The village is 40km from the nearest logging town as the crow flies. There are no roads leading to the village, and therefore, market integration is limited (Lew-Levy JRAI). In this community, BaYaka spend three to six months of the year in the village, where they do domestic and subsistence work for neighbouring Bandongo fisher-farmers (Boyette, Kandza), and make day and overnight trips into the forest to hunt and gather. The remaining year, BaYaka live in forest camps where they collect honey, seeds, nuts, tubers, fruits, greens, caterpillars, and mushrooms; hunt with spears or shotguns; trap; and fish in forest ponds (Kitanishi 1995). BaYaka also maintain low-input horticultural gardens of cassava and plantains, which they use to supplement their diets. 

**Data collection**  
Data was collected between July 23rd and September 2nd 2018 in the tropical wet season, during which caterpillar collecting and bail fishing were the primary subsistence activities (Jang and Boyette 2021; Mabossy-Mobouna et al. 2016). 52 individuals lived in camp (49% girls/women; mean age = 23.3, SD = 17.5, range = 2-70 years). BaYaka do not know their age in years; we estimated age using the methods outlined in Diekmann et al. The 42-day study period captured all residents continuously. Participants travelled an average of X km (SD=X) a day to forage. 

BaYaka camps are dense, with all residents fully visible to each other (Hewlett). YRO was stationed in camp, from where he could clearly see all individuals. He recorded the departure and return date and time for each individual. We considered any day on which an individual was out of camp—from short excursions to multi-day trips—as contributing to their foraging effort. 

YRO weighed foraging returns using a hanging spring scale, noting (1) the type of resource collected (e.g., porcupine, *koko* leaves), (2) the degree to which it was processed (e.g., whole, husked) (2) the specific container (e.g., basket, bucket) it was collected in, (3) the individuals who contributed to the food package, and (4) whether the item was a gift given from a non-camp-member (e.g., a visitor). We subtracted the empty container weight to produce a final field (or wet) weight for each item. Many items are stored for transport in *Marantaceae* leaves (Figure X); in these cases, the weight of the leaves could not be separated from that of the items.

YRO also asked participants to recall any food they had collected and eaten while out of camp, and the quantities of such items (e.g., two *maondo* fruit, 1L cup of palm wine). We estimated the field weight of each individual item from food returns weighed in camp, or from USDA FoodData Central (e.g., the average weight of a medium banana). 

Energetic estimates (kilocalories/gram) were calculated following best practices in nutritional anthropology (schnorr) as detailed in Benoit and colleagues. Briefly, information about inedible portions, moisture content, and energetic values were sourced from the published literature by LB. After subtracting inedible parts, she estimated the amount of kcals per gram of wet matter of a given food item for each processing condition, i.e.,  (1-Proportion\_Inedible) X kcal/g\_wet. This final value was multiplied with field weights to estimate total kcals.

**Statistical analysis**

We modeled foraging behavior using a Bayesian hierarchical framework with three interconnected components: (1) a binary effort model for daily participation, (2) a binary success model for returning with food, and (3) a Gamma model for group-level returns. Individual foraging skill was modeled as an age-dependent curve capturing both learning (skill increasing from birth) and senescence (skill declining in later life). To account for the cooperative nature of foraging, group returns were modeled as a function of the skills of participating individuals using a "best-top-k" aggregation that identifies the optimal subset of foragers contributing to group success. We estimated correlated random effects for effort propensity and skill, allowing us to test whether lower-skilled individuals compensate through increased effort. Temporal Gaussian processes captured day-to-day variation in foraging conditions. Models were fit using Markov Chain Monte Carlo via PyMC (version 5.x) and nutpie. We report posterior means and 95% credible intervals throughout.
